Why pilots stall, and what gets them to production

Five reasons promising pilots never reach production, what “production-ready” actually means, and ten questions to answer before you fund the next one.

OzAI Digital Published 22 March 2026 Updated 29 October 2026 6 min read
Updated 29 October 2026

Corrected. The earlier version opened with an “80 per cent of AI projects” figure attributed to Gartner that we could not trace. It is replaced with Gartner’s sourced July 2024 prediction. The checklist now ties to acceptance tests and ongoing operation.

Many pilots start with excitement, produce a promising demo, and then quietly stall. The pilot works in a controlled setting but never reaches production. The budget runs out, the team moves on, and the organisation is left with a proof of concept that proved only that the hard part was still ahead.

This is common enough that analysts forecast it. In July 2024 Gartner predicted that at least 30 per cent of generative AI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, citing poor data quality, inadequate risk controls, escalating costs and unclear business value. That was a prediction, not a measurement, but the reasons it gives match what we see. Understanding why pilots stall, and what to do differently, is the difference between a system people rely on and an expensive experiment.

Five reasons pilots stall

1. No clear business case

Many pilots were never built to solve a specific business problem. They were built because an executive read an article, a vendor offered a free proof of concept, or the innovation team needed something for the next board meeting.

When a pilot exists to show off technology rather than fix a measurable problem, there is no natural route to production. Nobody owns the outcome and nobody can put a number on its value. When it is time to fund the move to production, which always costs more than the pilot, there is no case to make.

The fix is simple to state: start with the business problem, the expected value and how you will measure success. If you cannot write these down, you are not ready for a pilot.

2. Data that is not production-ready

Pilots usually run on curated data. The team picks a sample, cleans it, formats it and gets impressive results. Then the system meets real production data and falls over.

Real data is messy: missing fields, inconsistent formats, duplicates, stale records and edge cases the demo never saw. A model that did well on the clean sample produces unreliable results on the real thing, and confidence evaporates.

Production needs production-ready data. That means investing in quality, building pipelines that cope with real conditions, and testing against genuinely representative samples, including the ugly ones, before calling the pilot a success.

3. No integration plan

A surprising number of pilots are built as standalone tools: a notebook, a separate web page, a script someone runs by hand. They do not connect to the CRM, the ERP, the case management system or whatever staff work in every day.

So even when the output is useful, using it means copying data out of one system, pasting it into the tool, and pasting the result back. In practice people stop doing that. The tool sits unused and the pilot is called a failure, not because the model did not work, but because nobody designed it into the workflow.

Plan integration from day one. Know where the system fits in the user's day, which systems it must connect to, and what the data flow looks like end to end.

4. Change management ignored

Adoption is a people problem. You can build an accurate, well-integrated system and it will still fail if the people meant to use it do not trust it, do not understand it, or resist it.

Automation raises particular worries. People fear being replaced. They distrust outputs they cannot check. They have years of expertise and do not want to defer to a machine. If those concerns are not addressed early, adoption stays low however good the system is.

What helps: involve end users in the design, be honest about what the system can and cannot do, train people hands-on rather than handing over a guide, point out the cases where it clearly helps, and give users a way to report problems and see them fixed.

5. No owner after handover

Pilots are often built by vendors or innovation teams separate from the business unit that will own the result. When the pilot ends, the builders move on and the business is left with a system nobody knows how to maintain.

These systems are not static. Model behaviour drifts as the data changes, providers retire models, integrations break when upstream systems change, and new edge cases appear. Without an owner and the means to maintain it, even a successful pilot decays in production.

Before any pilot starts, decide who will run it in production, and make sure they have the skills, budget and mandate. If that cannot be arranged, reconsider the project, or arrange for someone to run it for you.

What production-ready means

A production-ready system is different from a working prototype. It meets a clear bar on five dimensions.

Reliability. It works consistently, handles edge cases, and fails safely on inputs outside its expected range.

Monitoring. You can see how it is performing (accuracy, latency, error rates, usage), and you hear about problems before your users do.

Security. It handles data appropriately, respects access controls and adds no new attack surface. Where it processes personal information, it meets the Privacy Act.

Scalability. It copes with production volumes, not ten requests a day during a pilot but hundreds or thousands across the business.

Maintainability. It is documented, the code is clean, and someone understands it well enough to fix issues, swap models and evolve it.

A pilot that does not address all five is a demo, not a path to production.

Build for production from the start

We do not build pilots that have to be rebuilt for production. Every workflow build starts with a one-page scope, a fixed price and written acceptance tests covering inputs (including messy real cases), outputs, exceptions, where people approve, cost and latency thresholds, and the audit trail. We build against those tests on your real data, train the people who use it, and hand over the source, configuration and an acceptance record. If you want, we keep it running afterwards under a Run agreement: we watch it, re-test it when prompts or models change, and make the small fixes that keep it useful.

That costs a little more up front than a quick proof of concept. It avoids the much larger cost of a pilot that works in a demo and fails in production.

Ten questions to ask before you approve a project

Before you approve the next project, ask these. If you cannot answer them clearly, it is not ready.

  1. What specific business problem does this solve, and what acceptance tests will show it is solved?
  2. What value do we expect, and over what timeframe?
  3. Is the data we need available, clean enough and representative of real conditions, including the messy cases?
  4. How will this fit into existing workflows and systems?
  5. Who will use it day to day, and have they been involved in the design?
  6. What happens when it gets something wrong: what are the failure modes and the fallback?
  7. Where does a person approve before anything reaches a customer or a system of record?
  8. Who will run it in production, and how will we monitor performance and detect drift?
  9. Does it make or inform significant decisions about individuals, and if so, what must our privacy policy say?
  10. Who is responsible for adoption, and how will we know it is being used?

If you can answer all ten with confidence, the project is worth funding. If not, spend the effort answering them before you spend it building.

Moving forward

The gap between pilot and production is real, but not inevitable. Start with a clear business case, build on real data, design for integration and adoption, write the acceptance tests first, and decide who runs it before you start.
