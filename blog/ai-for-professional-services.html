AI in professional services: where it helps and where it doesn’t

Document review, drafting, knowledge management and time recording are good fits. Judgement, unsourced answers and client confidentiality need care.

OzAI Digital Published 10 March 2026 Updated 29 October 2026 4 min read
Updated 29 October 2026

Updated. Retitled; removed unsourced productivity percentages and an unverified client example; added a section on where the tools do not help or need care.

Professional services firms, including law firms, management consultancies, accounting practices and engineering consultancies, are natural candidates for language models. They sell expertise and time, and much of their work is reading, finding, summarising and drafting text. That makes it easy to overestimate what the tools do well, and easy to miss where they quietly fail.

Here is where we think the technology helps in professional services today, and where it does not.

Document review and analysis

This is where the value is most immediate. A retrieval-based assistant can search across precedents, contracts, policies and matter files and return the relevant passages, with links to the source, in seconds rather than hours.

Consider a contract repository. Instead of opening dozens of agreements to find a clause, a lawyer asks, "Which of our client contracts contain change-of-control provisions?" and gets a list of contracts with the relevant clauses quoted and linked. The lawyer still reads the clauses and forms the view; the assistant removes the hunt.

Consultancies can apply the same approach to proposal libraries, project archives and methodology documents. When a partner wants to see how the firm handled a similar engagement three years ago, the assistant finds and summarises the material.

Client communication

Drafting assistance is becoming normal practice. That does not mean sending generated emails unreviewed. It means producing a solid first draft that the professional then corrects and makes their own.

Meeting summaries are a strong use. After a client meeting, a structured summary with decisions, action items and follow-ups can be ready within minutes. A follow-up email can be drafted from the same notes, with agreed next steps and the relevant attachments. The professional reviews and personalises it; the assembly work is done.

For law firms, routine correspondence such as initial advice letters, standard updates and procedural letters is a good candidate, which leaves more of a lawyer's writing time for argument and strategic advice.

Knowledge management

Every firm has years of institutional knowledge in the heads of senior people, on file servers and in email. When a senior partner retires, much of it leaves too.

Indexing historical documents, internal wikis and recorded presentations makes that knowledge searchable. A graduate can ask a question and get an answer grounded in the firm's own past work, with links to the documents it came from.

This helps multi-office firms in particular. Knowledge siloed in one office or practice group becomes available across the firm, which improves consistency and reduces duplicated effort.

Time recording

Time recording is unpopular, and missed or rounded-down entries are a familiar source of lost revenue.

Tools can now suggest time entries from the day's activity (emails sent, documents opened, meetings attended) for the professional to review and approve rather than create from scratch. They can also suggest the matter, phase and task code for each entry, which reduces miscoding and write-offs. How much this recovers varies by firm and practice; measure it on your own data before and after rather than relying on vendor figures.

Risk and compliance

Contract review against a firm's standard positions is one of the more mature uses. A tool can flag unusual or missing clauses and highlight risks for a reviewer. It does not replace legal judgement, but it gives a junior reviewer a thorough starting point and reduces the chance that something is missed.

Regulatory change monitoring is another. Firms can track changes in legislation, regulatory guidance and case law and flag which clients or matters may be affected, for a professional to assess. With privacy, financial services and employment law all in active reform in Australia, that is useful.

Where it doesn't help, or needs care

Judgement. The tools are good at finding and drafting and poor at deciding what matters. Advice, strategy and risk calls stay with the professional.

Unverifiable output. A fluent answer with no source is a liability in professional work. Prefer setups where every answer links to the document it came from, and where the assistant says so when it cannot find support.

Confidentiality and privilege. Client material carries obligations. Know where each tool stores and processes data, whether it is used for training, and who can see what. Keep matter-level access controls in the retrieval layer, not just in the user interface.

Decisions about people. If a tool informs significant decisions about individuals, such as hiring or credit, the Privacy Act's new transparency obligation from 10 December 2026 may apply. See what changes on 10 December 2026.

The change management challenge

The technology is not the hard part. Getting experienced professionals to change how they work is. Partners who have succeeded for decades with their current methods are reasonably sceptical of tools that promise to do things differently.

What tends to work: start with a task people actively dislike, not an impressive demo. Involve senior practitioners in the design so the tool reflects how the work is really done. Measure the results and share them, so sceptics see evidence rather than claims.

Mandatory training and top-down mandates rarely work on their own. Making the tool genuinely useful, then expanding from there, does.

What results to expect

The realistic gains are faster review of document-heavy tasks, quicker first drafts of routine material, easier discovery of the firm's own past work, and more complete time records. The size of each gain depends on the firm, the practice and the quality of the underlying documents, so set a baseline before you start and measure against it.

The aggregate effect is not that firms need fewer professionals. Each professional spends less time on administration and more on the complex work clients pay for.
