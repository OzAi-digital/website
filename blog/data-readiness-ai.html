Your AI strategy will fail without data readiness

Most projects stall on data, not models. Four pillars of data readiness, the problems that stall projects, and a checklist to run before you build.

OzAI Digital Published 5 March 2026 Updated 29 October 2026 5 min read
Updated 29 October 2026

Updated. Softened unsourced claims to our own view and added a sourced reference; replaced the “30 days” framing with a checklist tied to acceptance-test inputs; added the Privacy Act transparency obligation from 10 December 2026.

In our experience, most projects of this kind do not fail because of the model. They fail because of the data: poor quality, formats that resist automation, unclear ownership, missing governance. Gartner's 2024 prediction that many generative AI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept named poor data quality first among its reasons. Yet many organisations go straight to choosing a model and designing the use case without checking whether their data can support it.

Data readiness is not glamorous and does not make for an exciting board paper. But it is, in our view, the most reliable early signal of whether a project will reach production or stall as an expensive pilot.

Four pillars of data readiness

1. Data quality

Language models are unforgiving of bad data. A knowledge assistant built on documents with inconsistent formatting, outdated content and duplicates will give unreliable answers, and unreliable answers destroy trust faster than anything else.

Quality has four dimensions. Completeness: are the fields filled in? Accuracy: does the data reflect reality, or has it drifted? Consistency: is the same thing represented the same way across systems? Timeliness: is it current enough for the decisions it supports?

You do not need perfect data to start. You do need to know where the problems are, and have a plan for the ones that matter to your use case.

2. Data accessibility

Data that cannot be reached might as well not exist. In most organisations, important information is spread across CRMs, ERPs, file shares, email archives, SharePoint sites, legacy databases and the occasional spreadsheet on someone's desktop.

Automated workflows need programmatic access, ideally through APIs or well-structured database connections. If the only way to get the data is a manual export, screen scraping or asking someone in finance to email a CSV every Monday, that will bottleneck everything you build on it.

The test: can we get the data we need, in a usable format, automatically, within an acceptable time? If not, for any critical source, fix that before, or alongside, the build.

3. Data governance

Governance answers the questions that matter when something goes wrong. Who owns this data? Where did it come from? Who may access it? What privacy classification does it carry? How long must it be kept?

For these systems, governance matters even more, because outputs are only as trustworthy as their inputs. If an assistant answers from a document, you need to trace the answer to that source, know who wrote it, confirm it is current, and check that the person asking is allowed to see it.

Australian organisations also have obligations under the Privacy Act 1988. Personal information used in these systems must be handled under the Australian Privacy Principles, with clear processes covering purpose, use and disclosure, and individuals' rights of access and correction. From 10 December 2026, if a program makes or substantially informs significant decisions about people, your privacy policy must also describe the kinds of personal information used and the kinds of decisions involved. See what changes on 10 December 2026.

4. Data infrastructure

These workloads need slightly different infrastructure from traditional reporting. Semantic search needs a vector index. Pipelines must generate embeddings and keep indexes current. Storage must handle structured records and unstructured documents: PDFs, emails, images, transcripts.

None of it needs to be complicated. A Postgres database with the pgvector extension, a straightforward ingestion pipeline and cloud storage for source documents can support a capable system. But it does need to exist, and be designed for the workload.

Common data problems that stall projects

Dirty data. Inconsistent naming, duplicate records, stale entries and free-text fields that resist automated processing. Dirty inputs produce dirty outputs.

No single source of truth. When the same fact has different values in different systems, such as a customer's address in the CRM and the billing system, the system cannot tell which is right. Outputs become inconsistent and trust erodes.

Regulatory constraints. Data that cannot be used for the purpose because of privacy restrictions, contract terms or consent gaps. Finding this after you have built is expensive. Finding it before you start is good planning.

Siloed ownership. Data controlled by different departments with no shared standards or integration layer. Breaking down silos is as much a political job as a technical one.

A readiness checklist

Data readiness does not need a two-year programme before you start. Work through this list for the one workflow you want to build first.

  • Pick the source that matters most. Identify the data set that, if ready, would support your most valuable workflow. Start there rather than trying to fix everything.
  • Collect real samples. Gather a set of real inputs, including the messy, incomplete and unusual ones. These become the "Inputs" part of your acceptance tests.
  • Profile quality. Measure completeness, duplicates and format consistency, and quantify the problems so you can fix them in order of impact.
  • Confirm automated access. Make sure the source can be read by an API or scheduled extract. A nightly export in a structured format is a real improvement over manual steps.
  • Record ownership and sensitivity. Note who owns each source, its privacy classification, and the legal basis for using it in this workflow.
  • Know where it will be processed. List each service that will store or process the data, and its region.

The data audit

For larger programmes, a structured audit is an efficient way to see where you stand across all four pillars. It maps what data exists, where it lives, how it is reached, who owns it and what condition it is in, then assesses each source and produces a prioritised list of fixes for the workflows you want to build.

It involves people from IT, data teams, the business and legal or compliance. The output should be a concrete plan with owners, timelines and dependencies, not a theoretical framework.

Privacy and data residency

For Australian organisations, data readiness and regulatory compliance cannot be separated. Strong data governance now makes it far easier to meet the Privacy Act's new automated-decision transparency obligation and any later reforms. Retrofitting it into systems not designed for it is harder.

Data residency matters just as much. Knowing where data is stored, processed and sent is a prerequisite for any build, and a design decision that shapes architecture, vendor choice and cost.
