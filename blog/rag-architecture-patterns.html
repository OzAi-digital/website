Retrieval patterns for knowledge assistants

Four ways to ground a language model in your own documents, from simple to involved, and how to choose between them without losing track of where your data goes.

OzAI Digital Published 28 March 2026 Updated 29 October 2026 4 min read
Updated 29 October 2026

Updated. Retitled; removed framework and vendor recommendations in favour of the patterns themselves; replaced blanket regional-availability claims with advice to check in-region processing for the specific model.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has become the most practical way to ground a language model in an organisation's own information. Rather than fine-tuning a model or relying on what it learned in training, a retrieval system finds relevant documents at question time and passes them to the model as context. Done well, answers are current, specific and traceable to a source.

Retrieval is attractive because your documents stay in systems you control while the model does the reading and writing. But implementations differ. The pattern you choose decides how well the system handles complex questions, how reliably it finds the right material, and what it does when the answer is not there.

Here are four patterns, from simple to more involved.

Pattern 1: Basic retrieval

The basic pipeline is simple: turn the question into a vector embedding, search an index for the most similar document chunks, and pass those chunks to the model with the question. The model answers from the retrieved context.

This suits FAQ systems, internal knowledge bases and support assistants where questions map fairly directly to existing documents. It needs only a document store, an embedding step and a vector index. A vector extension on a database you already run, such as pgvector on Postgres, or a managed index such as Cloudflare Vectorize, is usually enough.

The limitation is that every question is handled the same way. The system always retrieves, always from the same place, and never checks whether what it found actually answers the question. For simple, well-scoped uses that is fine. For anything more complex, you need more.

Pattern 2: Routed (agentic) retrieval

Routed retrieval puts a reasoning step in front of the search. Instead of searching every time, the model first decides whether retrieval is needed, what to search for, where to look, and how to combine results from several sources.

Suppose an employee asks, "What is our leave policy for part-time staff in Victoria?" A routed system might search the HR policy set, then the relevant employment-law reference, and combine both in its answer. It can also decide that a general question needs no retrieval at all.

This pattern fits organisations with several knowledge bases, structured databases and APIs. The router chooses the right source for each question. Each source is exposed as a narrow tool, which also gives you a natural place to log what was searched.

Pattern 3: Corrective retrieval

Corrective retrieval adds a check after the search. A separate evaluation step, often another model call, judges whether the retrieved material is relevant and sufficient. If not, the system searches again with a reformulated query, tries another source, or declines to answer.

This markedly reduces confident wrong answers. In legal or compliance settings, a confidently wrong answer is far worse than none. Corrective retrieval lets the system say, "I did not find enough to answer this reliably," or try another strategy first.

The cost is latency, because each check takes time. For interactive uses, balance thoroughness against speed. For back-office or batch work, the delay is usually acceptable.

Pattern 4: Graph-assisted retrieval

Graph-assisted retrieval combines vector search with a knowledge graph. Vector search is good at finding similar text but weak at questions about relationships between things: "Which suppliers have contracts expiring this quarter and open compliance issues?"

A knowledge graph stores entities (suppliers, contracts, compliance records) and their relationships explicitly. The system queries both the graph and the vector index and merges the results before answering. This helps in domains with heavily connected data: supply chains, regulatory compliance, financial services, healthcare.

Building and maintaining a graph takes more work up front. Entities and relationships must be extracted, checked and kept current. Where the relationships matter, it answers questions the simpler patterns cannot.

Data residency

Whichever pattern you choose, you need to know where data is stored, processed and sent. Under the Privacy Act and sector rules, that is a design decision, not an afterthought.

The major cloud providers all run Australian regions, so document stores, vector indexes and graphs can stay in Australia. Model inference is where care is needed. Whether a given model processes data in an Australian region varies by provider and by model, and changes over time. Check the provider's current in-region processing list for the specific model before you commit; Google, for example, publishes one per model for Vertex AI. Where requirements are stricter, an open-weight model hosted in your own environment is an option.

Map the whole flow: where documents are ingested, where embeddings are stored, where inference happens, and where questions and answers are logged. Each step needs to meet your residency requirements.

Choosing a pattern

Start with the simplest pattern that meets your needs. Basic retrieval suits well-scoped, single-source uses. Move to routed retrieval when questions span several sources or need different strategies. Add a corrective check when accuracy matters and wrong answers are costly. Consider a graph when your data has relationships that similarity search cannot capture.

Many real systems combine them: basic retrieval for straightforward lookups, routing for complex questions, and a corrective check on high-stakes answers, all in one application.
