Choosing a model: test it on your work, not a leaderboard

Model rankings change every few months. A method that lasts: test candidates on your own work, compare cost per accepted output, and check where each model processes data.

OzAI Digital Published 15 March 2026 Updated 29 October 2026 4 min read
Updated 29 October 2026

Rewritten. The earlier version compared named models, context windows and price tiers that are now out of date. This version is model-agnostic and describes how we evaluate candidates for a specific workflow.

An earlier version of this post compared specific models by name, with their context windows and price positions. Within months most of those figures were out of date. That is the point worth making: model rankings change every few months, so any choice built on a league table has a short shelf life. What lasts is the method.

This is how we choose a model for a workflow.

Start from the job, not the model

Every model has strengths. Some are better at careful reasoning over long documents, some at following a strict output format, some at speed and low cost. Some are available as open-weight models you can host yourself. None is best at everything, and the differences that matter for your workflow rarely show up in headline benchmarks.

So the choice should start from the work: what goes in, what must come out, how fast, how often, and what happens when the answer is wrong.

Test candidates against your acceptance tests

Before we build, we agree acceptance tests: approved sample inputs, including the messy cases, and the exact outputs the workflow must produce. Those same tests are the best model evaluation you can run.

Pick two or three candidates that are plausible on capability, cost and data location. Run each against the full set of samples. Score the outputs the same way you will at acceptance. The model that passes on your documents, your formats and your edge cases is the right one, whatever a leaderboard says.

What to compare

Accuracy on your data. For high-stakes work such as legal analysis, financial reporting or anything a customer will see, you need outputs that are correct and traceable to their source. Measure it on a representative sample of your real workload.

Cost per accepted output. Token prices are only part of the cost. A cheaper model that fails one case in five, and sends those cases to a person, can cost more than a dearer one that passes. Divide the total running cost by the number of outputs that pass your tests.

Latency. Interactive uses such as reception or enquiry chat need quick responses. Overnight batch work such as document classification can tolerate slower, cheaper processing.

Context needs. If the workflow reads long contracts, reports or codebases, check how each candidate handles your longest real inputs, not the advertised maximum. For many business tasks, a modest context is enough once retrieval is designed well.

Where data is processed. Know where prompts and outputs are processed and stored, whether they are used for training, and what the provider commits to contractually. This is often the deciding factor for Australian organisations.

Hosted or self-hosted

Hosted models through a managed API need no infrastructure work. You pay per use and get the provider's latest models. The trade-off is dependence on the provider and less control over data handling, though enterprise agreements usually include firm commitments on training and retention.

Open-weight models you host yourself give you full control: data stays in your environment and there are no per-token fees. The trade-off is that you need the engineering capacity to deploy, tune and maintain them, and the hardware cost is yours. For strict data-control requirements or very high volumes, they are worth evaluating.

For most mid-sized Australian businesses, hosted models through a major cloud platform offer the best balance of capability, simplicity and data control. The gap to open-weight models has been narrowing, so it is worth re-checking periodically.

Use more than one model where it pays

You do not have to pick a single model. Many workflows route different steps to different models: a small, fast model to classify incoming items, a stronger one for the few cases that need careful reasoning or drafting.

That adds a routing step and more to test, so do it only when the cost or speed difference is real for your volumes. Your acceptance tests tell you whether the cheaper route still passes.

Data residency: check per model, per region

For organisations subject to the Privacy Act, sector rules or government procurement requirements, where processing happens is critical.

The major cloud platforms run Australian regions, but a region being available does not mean every model processes data there. Availability and in-region processing vary by model and change over time. Check the provider's current list for the specific model you plan to use. Google Cloud, for example, publishes a per-model data residency list for Vertex AI, including which models offer in-region processing in Sydney (australia-southeast1).

For the most sensitive workloads, self-hosting an open-weight model inside your own environment removes the dependency on a provider's regional coverage.

Re-run the evaluation when models change

Providers release new models and retire old ones on their own schedules. Keep your acceptance tests as a regression suite. When a new model appears, or your current one is due to be retired, re-run the suite and compare. Switching then becomes a measured change rather than a leap of faith.
