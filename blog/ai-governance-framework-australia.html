Building an AI governance framework for Australian organisations

A practical, six-pillar framework that is proportionate to risk, consistent with current Australian obligations, and usable by the teams who build and run the systems.

OzAI Digital Published 27 March 2026 Updated 29 October 2026 6 min read
Updated 29 October 2026

Governance of automated systems has moved from a "nice to have" to a practical priority for Australian organisations. A new Privacy Act transparency obligation for automated decisions starts on 10 December 2026. The Australian Government has published guidance on how organisations should adopt AI responsibly. And boards, customers and auditors are asking the same question: how are you governing the systems you run?

Many organisations still lack a coherent answer. Some have high-level principles that sit in a document nobody reads. Others have processes so onerous that teams route around them. Neither works.

This post outlines a six-pillar framework that an Australian organisation can put in place this quarter: proportionate to risk, consistent with current obligations, and usable by the teams building and running the systems.

Why governance matters now

Three developments have made this pressing.

First, from 10 December 2026, organisations covered by the Privacy Act must state in their privacy policy the kinds of personal information used by computer programs that make, or substantially and directly inform, decisions that could significantly affect individuals, and the kinds of decisions involved (APPs 1.7 to 1.9, inserted by the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024). It is a disclosure obligation, and you cannot write the disclosure without knowing which systems you run. We cover the detail in Privacy Act automated-decision transparency: what changes on 10 December 2026.

Second, the policy settings have become clearer. In October 2025 the National AI Centre published the Australian Government's Guidance for AI Adoption, which replaced the Voluntary AI Safety Standard as the main reference for organisations. In December 2025 the National AI Plan confirmed that the Government would not proceed with the mandatory guardrails it had consulted on, and would rely on existing, technology-neutral laws and regulators, supported by a new AI Safety Institute. There is no AI-specific statute to comply with. Privacy, consumer, anti-discrimination, employment and sector-specific laws all still apply, and voluntary guidance shows what regulators and customers will expect.

Third, most organisations now have several AI-enabled systems in production or development. Without governance, each one is built with its own assumptions about data handling, risk and accountability, which produces inconsistency, duplication and gaps.

The six pillars

1. Policy and principles

Every framework starts with a clear statement of how your organisation will and will not use these systems. Not a statement about "responsible AI" in the abstract, but a policy that guides real decisions.

It should define the use cases you will and will not pursue, the boundaries you commit to (for example, no covert monitoring of staff), how the policy relates to the Australian Government's Guidance for AI Adoption (October 2025), and who can approve a new deployment.

Keep it short. A three-page policy people read is worth more than a fifty-page one nobody opens.

2. Risk assessment

Not every system carries the same risk. A drafting tool that helps staff write emails is different from a system that approves loan applications. You need a consistent way to classify them.

Keep a register of every system: its purpose, the data it uses, who it affects and its risk class. For higher-risk systems, particularly those that make or inform decisions about individuals, run a fuller impact assessment covering potential harms, bias and failure modes.

The classification sets the level of rigour. Low-risk systems might need registration and periodic review. High-risk systems need documentation, testing, human oversight and regular audit.

3. Data governance

These systems are only as good as the data they consume, and weak data governance is behind many failures and compliance problems.

Cover data quality (accurate, complete, representative), data residency (where data is stored and processed, which matters when cloud services may process data overseas), and privacy (personal information used in a system was collected and is used consistently with the Australian Privacy Principles).

Pay particular attention to training and fine-tuning data. Set clear rules for what data can be used, how it is de-identified, and how individuals' rights are preserved.

4. Transparency and explainability

For decisions in scope of APPs 1.7 to 1.9, transparency is now a legal requirement, in the form of a privacy-policy disclosure. It is good practice for every system, not just those caught by the law.

For each system, document what it does and why it exists, its inputs, how its outputs are produced, its known limitations and failure modes, and how people should interpret its outputs.

The enacted obligation does not require you to explain individual decisions or offer a human-review pathway. Rights of that kind were discussed in the Privacy Act Review and may arrive in a later tranche. Designing for them now costs little, and it makes your privacy-policy disclosure easier to write accurately. If you cannot explain how a system reaches its outputs, question whether it should be used for decisions at all.

5. Accountability

Governance without named owners is governance in name only.

Set up an oversight group, usually senior people from technology, legal, risk and the business, that approves high-risk deployments and sets standards. Give each system an owner responsible for its performance, compliance and incident response. Define escalation paths for when a system produces unexpected or harmful results.

Accountability extends to third-party products. Include vendor assessment criteria, contract terms on transparency and data handling, and clear responsibility for monitoring what vendors supply. The OAIC has been explicit that buying a product does not move the privacy obligation to the vendor.

6. Monitoring and audit

Governance is not a one-off exercise. Data changes, operating environments change and obligations change.

Monitor performance (is it still doing what it should?), fairness (are outputs consistent across groups of people?), drift (has the input data shifted?) and compliance (does it still meet current obligations?).

Periodic audits, internal or external, check that the processes are followed and still fit for purpose. For high-risk systems, audit at least annually, or sooner if the system or its environment changes materially.

Common mistakes

The same mistakes come up repeatedly.

Treating governance as a project. It is an ongoing capability, not a deliverable. A framework built and then left alone is out of date within months. Assign permanent ownership and review cycles.

Over-engineering it. A framework that is too complex will be ignored. Start with the minimum that covers your highest risks and current obligations, then iterate. Complexity should match risk.

Leaving the business out. Frameworks written only by legal or compliance teams tend to be impractical. The people building and using the systems should help design the processes they will follow. If governance feels like an obstacle, the balance is wrong.

Starting from scratch. You do not need to. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework, ISO/IEC 42001 and the Australian Government's Guidance for AI Adoption (October 2025) are solid foundations. Adopt and adapt.

Getting started this quarter

If you have no framework yet, or one that exists only on paper, start here.

Take an inventory. Identify every system in use, including tools staff use without formal approval. You cannot govern what you do not know about.

Classify by risk. Start with systems that make or inform decisions about individuals. They are the ones the 10 December privacy-policy disclosure is about.

Write a short policy. Base it on the Government's Guidance for AI Adoption (October 2025) and get executive sign-off.

Name the owners. Appoint a governance lead, form a cross-functional oversight group, and assign an owner to each high-risk system.

Document the high-risk systems. For each, record its purpose, data, logic, limitations and controls.

These five steps will not give you a complete framework. They will give you a defensible foundation and a clear view of your exposure, which is what you need with 10 December close.
