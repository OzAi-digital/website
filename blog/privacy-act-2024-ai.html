Privacy Act automated-decision transparency: what changes on 10 December 2026

From 10 December 2026, organisations covered by the Privacy Act must describe in their privacy policy when computer programs make, or substantially inform, significant decisions about people. Here is what the law requires, what it does not, and how to prepare.

Updated 29 October 2026

On 10 December 2026 a new transparency obligation in the Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) takes effect. Organisations covered by the Act will have to say, in their privacy policy, when computer programs make, or substantially help make, decisions that could significantly affect people. It sounds narrow, and in one sense it is: it is a disclosure obligation, not a ban and not a new review process. But the scope is wider than many teams assume, and it reaches ordinary software as well as machine learning.

This post sets out what the law requires, what it does not, and what to do in the weeks that remain.

Which law, and when

The obligation comes from the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024, which received Royal Assent on 10 December 2024. It was the first tranche of the Government's response to the Privacy Act Review. Most of its changes started soon after assent. The automated decision-making provisions, which insert new Australian Privacy Principles (APPs) 1.7 to 1.9, start 24 months after assent: 10 December 2026. They apply to decisions made on or after that date.

In September 2026 the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) published guidance resources on the new obligation, including a fact sheet and a flowchart. Most of what follows draws on those documents.

When the obligation applies

According to the OAIC, an organisation is in scope when all three of these are true:

  • it has arranged for a computer program to make a decision, or to do a thing that is substantially and directly related to making a decision;
  • the decision could reasonably be expected to significantly affect the rights or interests of an individual; and
  • personal information about that individual is used in the operation of the program.

"Computer program" is broad. The OAIC's list includes pre-programmed rule-based processes, machine learning, ordinary software and apps, and generative tools used to produce text, images or code, including chatbots. "Arranged for" is broad too: building software in-house, buying a third-party product, configuring off-the-shelf software, and relying on a program's advisory output all count. Using a vendor's system does not move the obligation to the vendor.

Decisions commonly cited as potentially significant include:

  • credit applications and loan approvals
  • insurance pricing and claims assessments
  • employment screening and recruitment shortlisting
  • eligibility for products or services
  • account suspensions and similar account actions
  • government benefit determinations

Whether a particular decision is "significant" depends on its effect on the person. The OAIC notes that the impact must be more than trivial, and that a decision may weigh more heavily on someone experiencing vulnerability.

What you must disclose

If you are in scope, your APP privacy policy must describe:

  1. the kinds of personal information used in the operation of those programs;
  2. the kinds of decisions made solely by the operation of those programs; and
  3. the kinds of decisions for which a program does something substantially and directly related to making the decision.

Commercially sensitive information and trade secrets about the programs are excluded. Information that is merely embarrassing is not.

An earlier version of this post said organisations would have to "explain the logic involved" and "provide a pathway for human review". That was wrong. Rights of that kind were discussed during the Privacy Act Review and may come in a later tranche of reform, but they are not in the law that starts on 10 December 2026.

Human review does not take you out of scope

A common assumption is that a program which only recommends, with a person making the final call, is not an automated decision. The OAIC's guidance does not support that.

A decision may be within scope of the transparency obligation even where a computer program output does not replace the entire decision-making process or is subject to human review.

OAIC, APP 1.7–1.9 Transparency Obligation Fact Sheet

The OAIC says a program can be substantially and directly related to a decision whether its output is advisory or determinative. It considers that machine learning or generative outputs used to make significant decisions would generally be in scope unless they are subject to extensive human oversight and control. If staff routinely accept a recommendation without assessing it themselves, you should expect the decision to be in scope.

Tools used purely for internal analytics or drafting, which do not feed decisions about identifiable people, are less likely to be caught. Where you are unsure, the OAIC's advice is to take a cautious approach and include the information in your privacy policy.

Penalties: what is new and what is not

The headline maximum penalty for serious or repeated interferences with privacy, for a company the greater of $50 million, three times the benefit obtained, or 30 per cent of adjusted turnover in the relevant period, is not new. It came from the Privacy Legislation Amendment (Enforcement and Other Measures) Act 2022, which commenced on 13 December 2022.

The 2024 Act added lower tiers beneath it: a mid-tier civil penalty for interferences with privacy that are not serious, and an infringement-notice regime the OAIC can use for specified breaches. The practical effect is that smaller contraventions now carry a real cost too.

What to do before 10 December

List the programs. Identify every program that makes or informs decisions about individuals: in-house systems, purchased products, configured platforms, and the AI tools staff are permitted to use in decision-making. Chatbots that handle complaints, pricing engines and screening tools all belong on the list.

Apply the three-part test to each, and write down why. Record whether the program is substantially and directly related to a decision, whether the decision could significantly affect someone, and whether personal information is used. The record matters as much as the answer.

Ask your vendors. The OAIC expects providers of third-party software to give clear, high-level information about how their products can be used to make decisions. Ask for it now; you need it to write your policy.

Draft the privacy-policy text. Describe the kinds of personal information and the kinds of decisions, in plain language. Have your privacy adviser or lawyer review it.

Check the inputs. Confirm that the personal information feeding these programs was collected and is used consistently with the APPs. This is not new law, but the inventory tends to surface problems.

Go further where it makes sense. Documenting how each system reaches its outputs, and making human review genuine rather than a formality, are not required by APP 1.7–1.9. They are good practice, they make your disclosure easier to write accurately, and they put you in a better position if later reforms add individual rights.

Where governance frameworks help

A governance framework turns this from a one-off scramble into a routine. The useful parts are a register of systems with their risk classification, documented purpose, inputs and limitations for each, named owners, monitoring, an incident process, and clear escalation to people. Organisations already working with the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, ISO/IEC 42001, or the Australian Government's Guidance for AI Adoption (October 2025), which replaced the Voluntary AI Safety Standard, will find much of the inventory already done.
